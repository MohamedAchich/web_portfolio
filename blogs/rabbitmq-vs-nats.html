RabbitMQ vs. NATS: picking a broker for event-driven services

Every time a new service needs to talk to another service asynchronously, the same question comes up: RabbitMQ or NATS? Both are good tools solving overlapping but not identical problems, and the wrong pick usually shows up six months later as an operational headache rather than a day-one failure.

What each one optimizes for

RabbitMQ is a broker built around queues, exchanges, and routing rules — it's expressive about how a message gets to a consumer (fanout, topic, direct, dead-letter queues built in). NATS Core is built around raw throughput and simplicity — publish-subscribe with almost no ceremony. NATS JetStream adds persistence, replay, and at-least-once delivery on top, closing most of the feature gap with RabbitMQ while keeping the operational footprint lighter.

RabbitMQNATS (JetStream)
Delivery guaranteesAt-least-once, DLQs built inAt-least-once with JetStream, at-most-once on Core
Message replayRequires a plugin or app-level patternNative — replay by time or sequence
Routing modelRich (exchanges, bindings, topic matching)Subject hierarchies with wildcards
Operational footprintHeavier — Erlang VM, management pluginLightweight single binary, clusters easily

Where I've landed

For high-fanout device event pipelines — many publishers, many subscribers, and a need to replay a stream after a consumer was down — NATS JetStream has been the better fit. The subject-hierarchy model maps cleanly onto "events from device X, protocol Y" without building a routing layer by hand, and running a JetStream cluster is meaningfully less operational overhead than a RabbitMQ cluster with mirrored queues.

RabbitMQ still wins when the routing logic itself is the hard part — complex conditional fan-out, strict per-consumer acknowledgment semantics, or a team that already has deep Rabbit operational experience. Neither is "faster" in a way that matters until you're past the scale where this is your first bottleneck. Pick based on the shape of your routing problem, not a benchmark.
